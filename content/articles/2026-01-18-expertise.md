# Expertise is the art of ignoring

I've been writing code professionally for about 25 years now. The first eight in PHP, and the seventeen after that in Python. And somehow I felt more like an expert after eight years of PHP than I do after seventeen years of Python.

That's weird, right? I'm pretty sure that I didn't get worse at my job. Instead I think the idea of mastering a language simply stopped making sense.

Let's look at my years with PHP. Back in the early 2000s, if you knew strings, `$_POST`, sessions, and how to talk to a MySQL database, you basically knew PHP. Every developer built their own framework or CMS - I certainly did. The official manual was pretty small, and it was definitely possible to know the entire language. Or at least most of it.

Contrast that with modern Python. It's used for building web apps, embedded systems, automation scripts, and machine learning pipelines. The surface area of the language is so much bigger now! The manual is no longer something you could print and read, and it contains huge portions that I'll never need to touch. Whole modules I've never had to look at, and never will.

I think that's what makes me feel less like an expert, because I know less of the entire language. But the slice that I actually care about - this I know really well. I just have to keep that in mind whenever I feel imposter syndrome creeping up.

On top of this, I think modern frameworks hide much of the language from you. When I use Django, I mostly think in models, the ORM, and API endpoints, not so much Python. It's the same with relative newcomers to iOS who use SwiftUI to make mobile apps without knowing all the ins and outs of Swift, the language.

## Just-in-time beats just-in-case

So, here's a pro tip from a senior developer: don't feel like you need to learn everything up front just in case it's needed someday. I understand the urge, but it really isn't necessary.

Instead, focus on your slice of the language. When you write web apps with Python, you don't need to know how to write C extensions! Only learn what you actually need now, go deep where necessary, and ignore the rest.

Another reason to only learn what you need is that unapplied knowledge decays pretty fast. Whatever you learn now without actually using it in practice, you'll forget sooner than you'd like.

Seniority means knowing which details matter now, and what can wait. It's not about who knows the most things, but about knowing where to look up things when you need them.
