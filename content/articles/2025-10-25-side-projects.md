# My one rule for side projects

We all love a good side project. It's the place where you get to build things purely for the joy of it, where you can explore new tech, and maybe even stumble upon your next big idea.

But there's a subtle trap that I've walked into not once, but twice. Each time, it cost me a project I cared about.

## The rise and fall of my passion project

Back in 2001, my passion project was a music fan site. I loved working on this site. I spent countless hours building my own PHP-based CMS for it, and enjoyed every line of code, every new feature. It was pure fun.

In 2005 I got a new day job, and my professional life suddenly revolved around building websites with TYPO3, a powerful and notoriously complex open-source PHP CMS. After about a year, feeling like a TYPO3 guru, I had an idea: why not migrate my fan site over to TYPO3? It made perfect sense! I was working with it all day anyway.

Huge mistake.

What had been a joyful escape quickly became an extension of my workday. After eight hours wrangling TYPO3, coming home to do the exact same thing felt like a second shift. Instead of inventing new features, I was troubleshooting slow page loads and performance issues. The spark went out. One day, without planning or warning, I simply pulled the plug.

I didn't start another user-facing side project for almost fifteen years.

## History repeats itself

Fast forward to 2020. The itch returned, and I started something new: [critical-notes.com](https://www.critical-notes.com). I started with Svelte and Firebase, eventually moving the stack to SvelteKit and Django by late 2021. My day job was pure iOS development, so this side project was a completely different kind of experience. I loved working on this site.

Then in 2023 I decided to [pivot professionally](/articles/2025/thoughts-on-apple/) and return to the open web. My new client's project involved a SvelteKit frontend and a Django backend: the exact same technologies powering Critical Notes. "Brilliant!" I thought. "I can use my side project knowledge at work, and vice versa!"

Oh, how quickly we forget.

And indeed, the motivation began to fade. After a full day immersed in SvelteKit and Django at work, the thought of spending my free time tackling SvelteKit 5 updates, Tailwind migrations, or building new features for CN felt like, well, _work_. The struggle to find energy for something I used to love became very real.

## My advice

Have a side project. Absolutely. It gives you room to experiment, learn, and explore ideas you may never touch in your day job.

But please keep its tech stack different from the one you use at work.

Think of it like this: a professional furniture maker who builds chairs all day probably doesn't unwind by building another chair in the evening. They'd sketch, or carve something decorative, or pick up a different material entirely.

Don't let familiarity turn your side project into a chore. Let it be your escape, the place where you rediscover why building things is fun in the first place.
