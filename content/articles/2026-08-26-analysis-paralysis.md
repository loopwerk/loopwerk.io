# Analysis paralysis sucks

> Analysis paralysis describes a process where overanalyzing or overthinking a situation can cause decision-making to become "paralyzed", meaning that no solution or course of action is decided upon within a natural time frame. A situation may be deemed too complicated and a decision is never made, or made much too late, due to anxiety that a potentially larger problem may arise. A person may desire a perfect solution, but may fear making a decision that could result in error, while on the way to a better solution. Equally, a person may hold that a superior solution is a short step away, and stall in its endless pursuit, with no concept of diminishing returns.
> Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_paralysis)

I suffer from analysis paralysis when it comes to buying certain items, and by far the worst is when I want to buy a new camera. I'll end up with a spreadsheet where I have columns for all my must-have features and wishes, and rate every camera that I can think of. It takes me literally multiple weeks to make a decision, before I am finally convinced I found the best compromise (because the "perfect" camera doesn't exist). The problem is that I've worked on this for so long, the expectation is now so high, that my purchase rarely lives up to the hype and I end up being underwhelmed at best, disappointed at worst. And so the cycle starts again.

## It's blocking junior developers
I see the same analysis paralysis in developers I've mentored over the years, when it comes to getting started with a new language or framework. Like the person who didn't start because they didn't know how to organize code into different files. Or someone who didn't build because they didn't know how to write tests. 

These developers worry so much about "getting it right". About choosing the wrong architecture, or the wrong project setup, or any other "mistake", that they end up watching YouTube videos for days, getting lost in Reddit threads, instead of diving in and figuring out for themselves what works best.

The thing is that without building up this experience, it's almost impossible to know what the "right" way is. There's no single right answer that applies to every person and every project. You have to actually get started to figure out the pain points. And yes, you'll figure out what doesn't work at some point down the line, when you've already written a bunch of code. But you're not stuck with the wrong solution; code is easy enough to change. This is how you learn the craft, by making mistakes and fixing them. Research feels like progress without exposing you to failure. It's comfortable, but doesn't get you a job as a developer.

So, get started without overthinking! Don't worry about architecture. Put all the code in one file, who cares. You can organize your code later on once you've figured out how to do it, and once you've learned what makes sense for you. And here's a controversial opinion: don't worry about writing tests when you're starting out. I didn't do any unit testing for the first ten years of my career building web applications, instead always focusing on manual testing, and it was fine. 

I'm not saying you shouldn't learn how to write tests. But it's much more important to actually build stuff and learn the craft, and you'll learn the rest later. Don't let not knowing how to do something properly prevent you from doing it badly.

## Good enough should be good enough
It's not just junior developers at the beginning of their journey that get trapped by analysis paralysis; seniors are just better at rationalizing it. 

A senior developer knows about all the things that can go wrong, so they start designing for all those hypothetical problems before they've established that any of them are real problems. Too many abstractions because they "might need it later". Designing for a scale the project will almost certainly never reach, increasing complexity and the maintenance burden for no real benefit.

The junior developer doesn't start because they don't know enough. The senior developer doesn't start because they know too many things that could go wrong. Both are stuck.

I'm a huge believer in keeping things simple and being pragmatic. Good enough should be good enough. Not every decision deserves hours of research. Not every piece of code needs the perfect abstraction. And you don't need to be ready for a million users before you've even found your first thousand. There is a point where another day of research isn't going to make the outcome meaningfully better. That's diminishing returns, and it's something I have to remind myself of when I'm three weeks into researching which camera to buy. At some point, you just have to pick one and start using it.

The same applies to software. Make a reasonable decision. Build something, and see what happens. If it turns out you made the wrong decision, change it. You don't need to know the perfect way to do something before you start. Quite often, you can't know it. You have to get your hands dirty first. Analysis paralysis sucks. Don't let the search for the perfect solution stop you from making a good enough one.
